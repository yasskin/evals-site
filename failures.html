What went wrong, and which failures matter

A failure taxonomy sorts mistakes into types so a researcher can see which are worth fixing with data and which are noise. We split failures into two kinds: tasks where no usable file was produced, and errors inside files that were handed in.

In plain English

Not every failure says something about the model. A server timeout is noise. A checklist that contradicts its own task is a grading problem. A model that runs out of room before saving its work, or confidently writes the wrong case number, is a real gap a dataset could target. Each failure below is labeled with who or what was responsible and whether it matters to a researcher.

Part B: errors inside delivered files (hand-checked ledger of 30)

Hand-checked. Each item was first classified from both AI judges' cited evidence, then checked by hand against the original file: 21 confirmed as drafted, 7 reclassified and 2 rejected because the judges were wrong. One reviewer, so severities remain a judgment call. Download the full ledger (CSV) with task, run and checklist-item IDs, both judges' reasoning and the hand-check result.

How the 30 were chosen

  1. Start from agreementOnly checklist items that both judges failed. One judge alone is not enough.
  2. Find contrastsOn the 143 tasks where both models delivered a file: 67 tasks where both judges failed an item for GPT and passed it for Claude, 40 the reverse, and 86 where both failed.
  3. Prioritize weightHighest-weight item per task, so the errors that cost the most points come first.
  4. Spread and labelPicked 12 GPT, 12 Claude and 6 shared items across occupations, then labeled each one's type and severity.

Those counts compare the two models on the same tasks, but they do not include judge self-preference or unverifiable items, so read them as a direction, not a rate.

Three patterns worth building data for

1. Claude runs out of room before saving the file

Evidence: 76 of 220 tasks without a usable file; 13 used every tool call and then pasted the content as chat text. With larger limits, 53 of 62 cut-off tasks delivered.

Other explanation: the limits may simply be tight for Claude's default reasoning. The retry shows budget sensitivity, not a single cause.

Data that could help: worked examples that save a first draft early and then improve it, under explicit budgets.

Follow-up test: rerun the 76 tasks at the same limits; count files and grade them.

2. GPT's files look finished but miss specific requirements

Evidence: on 143 tasks where both delivered, both judges failed a GPT checklist item that they passed for Claude on 67 tasks, versus 40 the other way. Examples above include a wrong legal conclusion, invented return figures and a missing safety margin.

Other explanation: GPT ran with a low reasoning setting, and judges may prefer their own model's style.

Data that could help: requirement-dense tasks with item-by-item expert checklists and near-miss files paired with corrections.

Follow-up test: new held-out tasks scored by experts on critical omissions.

3. Both: the grading itself is a gap, so evaluation data has value too

Evidence: judges could not verify 24.3% of checklist items, including 98% of items on PowerPoint files. Two of the 30 ledger items are checklists that contradict their own task. Both models also failed the same items on 86 tasks, often ones needing maps, photos or careful formulas.

Data that could help: rendered gold-standard files, machine-checkable acceptance tests and audited checklists. These make an evaluation trustworthy for both labs before any training claim. See Can we trust the judges?

These patterns come from one try per task and AI grading. They suggest where data might help; they do not show that training on such data would improve either model.
