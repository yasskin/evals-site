Delivering a file is not the same as finishing the job.

GPT-6 Astra (with a low reasoning setting) finished and handed in a file on 211 of 220 tasks. The next step is making those files meet every requirement a professional would expect.

In plain English

GPT is very reliable at producing the requested file. But when the files are checked line by line against the task's checklist, they sometimes skip specific required details. A document can look polished and still be unusable at work if it misses a required safety step. The fix we suggest is training examples that teach the model to cover every stated requirement.

Why this dataset is worth buying, for evaluation and for training

As an evaluation: it measures what a delivery rate hides.

  • It finds gaps that "did it hand in a file?" misses. GPT delivered on 211 of 220 tasks, yet on 67 of the 143 tasks where both models delivered, both judges agreed a GPT checklist item failed that passed for Claude.
  • Its checklists can be graded automatically where a file can be read. Our two judges, from rival companies, agreed on 95.7% of pass/fail calls they could both check. That makes "did it cover every requirement?" a repeatable regression test across model versions.
  • It shows what still needs an expert. 24.3% of checklist items could not be verified automatically: mostly where no file was handed in, plus slides, video and ZIP files the judges could not open. An evaluation buyer gets a clear map of where automated scores can and cannot be trusted.

For training: it shows exactly what to teach.

  • The problem is quality, not delivery. GPT already hands in a file almost every time, so training should target what goes inside it, without trading away that reliability.
  • The misses that matter are judgment calls. In our 12-item GPT draft ledger, 5 are domain-judgment errors and 4 are high severity, such as a wrong legal bottom line or invented figures a board could act on.
  • The fix is concrete. Expert gold files, plus polished-but-incomplete drafts paired with their corrections, show the model exactly which requirements it tends to skip.

Example: a warehouse safety procedure

The task asked for an SOP (step-by-step procedure) for handling electronics that can be damaged by static electricity (ESD).

Both models delivered a Word document. Both judges scored Claude's at least 11.3 percentage points higher. Both judges found that GPT's document left out these requirements, which Claude's included:

  • Connecting the protected work area to a single shared ground point
  • Storing parts on grounded shelves or racks
  • Describing the symbol that marks static-protective packaging
  • Plain-language definitions of ESD and of parts that static can damage

We also read the original documents. GPT gave sound general advice on grounding but left out these specific requirements. See both files and every checklist verdict →

A similar pattern appeared in a journalism task: GPT delivered a Markdown text file instead of the requested PDF, and it left out context and figures the checklist required. These are individual examples; we have not measured how often this happens across all tasks.

Proposed dataset: cover every requirement

A training dataset proposal describes new examples that could teach the model to fix this weakness. This is a draft specification; sizes are starting points to test, not commitments.

Task mixRequirement-dense professional deliverables where a missed detail changes the decision: legal memos, engineering and safety procedures, deal documents, financial analyses and clinical or pharmacy guidance.
InputsRealistic instructions plus reference files (spreadsheets, PDFs, prior records) that contain the facts the answer must use.
Gold outputsExpert-written files in the requested format that satisfy every stated requirement.
Expert annotationsA requirement checklist that links each item to the part of the gold file that satisfies it, with a severity label (high / medium / low).
Negative / repair pairsPlausible but incomplete drafts (polished, yet missing a safety step, deal term or legal conclusion) paired with the expert's corrected version.
Use as an evaluationHold out a set of tasks as a completeness test: score each model version on critical omissions per file, alongside its delivery rate, so a gain in one cannot hide a loss in the other.
Acceptance testsMachine-checkable format and content rules where possible, plus expert review of the high-severity items.
Proposed sizeA pilot of about 300 tasks across the professions where GPT missed items most often, scaled only if the held-out test below improves.
Evidence so far29 of 220 tasks where both judges scored Claude clearly higher; 67 of 143 both-delivered tasks with a checklist item both judges failed for GPT and passed for Claude; 12 draft ledger items (5 domain judgment, 3 completeness, 2 quantitative). See Failures.
Other explanationsGPT ran with a low reasoning setting, so some misses may disappear at higher effort. AI judges are not yet checked against human experts.
Follow-up testRe-run the 29 tasks and new held-out tasks from the same occupations at a higher reasoning setting. Have experts count critical omissions. The dataset is worth building if omissions persist.
Training pitch: "Your model already delivers files reliably. The opportunity is making them operationally complete: covering the specific requirements that separate a plausible document from a usable one."
Evaluation pitch: "A delivery rate says your model is nearly done. These expert checklists show what it hides: on 67 of the 143 tasks where both models handed in a file, both judges found a requirement GPT missed and Claude met. Use it as a completeness score to track every model version."

How to tell it worked: experts find fewer critical omissions on new tasks, and the model still delivers files just as often.

GPT ran with a low reasoning setting; a higher setting might change these results. Quality results come from AI judges and are provisional. Meeting the checklist does not certify that a document is technically safe.
